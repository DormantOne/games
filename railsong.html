HOW THE BRAIN LEARNS

At every junction, each train tests legal exits through its team’s 37-input, 9-neuron recurrent policy, combining local food, enemy size, tail position, HP, vision, junction type and recent visits with the pitch of each candidate rail — its consonance against every note still ringing, its melodic interval from the train’s last note, its scale degree and register — plus its own persistent memory. Outputs score direction, speed, division and whether to sound the note.
After a round, the better-scoring squad’s complete weights are retained. A mutated copy challenges it on a new random track in a new key and mode, so a brain cannot memorise one tune; it has to learn intervals. All three starting agents and their offspring contribute to team fitness. This is evolutionary self-play, not backpropagation or a pretrained CNN.
Pitch is not random noise on the board. Move one rail sideways and the note rises a fifth in the scale; move one rail down and it falls a third. The board is a diatonic lattice with roughly one rail in six bent out of key, so triads sit under every junction and a train that learns to steer is a train that learns to sing.

CAPTAIN'S NOTES

The track is randomly generated but connected. Full four-way crossings are marked with luminous diamonds; watch long trains divide there. Rail colour is the scale degree, brightness is the octave, dusty magenta means an accidental. The evolutionary population is kept in this tab only.
